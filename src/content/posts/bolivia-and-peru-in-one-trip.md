The bus leaves the Peruvian lakeshore and climbs over a low rise, and suddenly the water is everywhere: Lake Titicaca, a hard bright blue under a huge sky. Soon you'll be queueing at a small border post with your passport in hand. Not long after, you're walking down to the waterfront in Copacabana, in a different country, looking out at the same lake.

Peru and Bolivia share the lake, the altiplano and a great deal of history, and linking them is one of the classic South American journeys. Cusco and Machu Picchu on one side; Titicaca, La Paz and the Uyuni salt flats on the other. Done in the right order, the route also helps your body adjust to the altitude. Here's how to plan it.

## The classic route

The usual overland line runs:

**Cusco → Puno → (border) → Copacabana → La Paz**

or the same in reverse. In outline:

- **Cusco and the Sacred Valley.** The old Inca capital, plus the valley below it, and the base for Machu Picchu.
- **Puno.** A lakeside city on the Peruvian shore of Titicaca, reached from Cusco by road or by tourist train.
- **Copacabana.** The Bolivian lake town and jumping-off point for Isla del Sol. Our guide to [Lake Titicaca from the Bolivian side](/blog/lake-titicaca-bolivian-side/) covers it in detail.
- **La Paz.** Bolivia's seat of government, with cable cars, markets and day trips. See [riding La Paz's cable cars](/blog/la-paz-cable-cars/).

From La Paz you can fly home, carry on south to Uyuni, or keep going into Chile.

## Crossing the border

There are two main road crossings between Puno and La Paz, both run on the Peruvian side by Peru's immigration authority, [Migraciones](https://www.gob.pe/institucion/migraciones/noticias/998396-puno-migraciones-fortalece-la-seguridad-y-el-control-migratorio-en-frontera-con-bolivia):

- **Kasani (Yunguyo).** Near Copacabana. This is the one to use if you want to stop at Copacabana and Isla del Sol.
- **Desaguadero.** At the southern end of the lake, on the road that goes straight between Puno and La Paz without passing Copacabana. It suits travellers who are skipping the lake towns.

At either crossing you walk through two sets of formalities: out of one country, then into the other. Whichever direction you're going, the most important thing is to **get both stamps**:

- The [UK FCDO says](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/entry-requirements) that if you enter Bolivia overland you should be stamped out of the country you're leaving and stamped into Bolivia. Without an entry stamp you'll be fined when you leave.
- For Peru, the [FCDO says](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/peru/entry-requirements) to get an entry stamp at the immigration checkpoint, and that you can't leave Peru without one.

Some safety points from official advice:

- The FCDO's [Bolivia safety page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) advises taking a direct bus between Copacabana and La Paz. It warns that people entering overland from Peru, Chile or Argentina are especially at risk of express kidnapping.
- [Canada's travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/bolivia) says to avoid crossing land borders at night, when border officials and police may not be present.
- Protests and road blockades happen on both sides. The FCDO's [Peru safety page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/peru/safety-and-security) names Puno and Cusco among regions with recent protests. It says protests can disrupt trains, including those to Machu Picchu, and can suspend immigration services at land borders. Build in a spare day and don't try to pass a blockade.

## Entry rules for both countries

Entry rules depend on your nationality and they change, so check the official pages for your passport before you book. As a starting point, here's what UK government advice said on the date we checked.

**Bolivia** ([FCDO entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/entry-requirements)):

- British visitors can stay up to 90 days without a visa. Border officers may stamp 30 days, but the FCDO says you can still stay the full 90 days without a new stamp.
- Your passport should be valid for at least six months from arrival, with at least two blank pages.
- You may be asked for proof of accommodation, such as a hotel booking and its address.
- You need a yellow fever certificate if you're arriving from a country with a risk of yellow fever transmission. Check [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/) for whether that applies to your route.

**Peru** ([FCDO entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/peru/entry-requirements)):

- British visitors normally get permission to stay up to 90 days in a 180-day period, and don't need a visa for tourism.
- Your passport must be valid for at least six months after the date you arrive.
- If you overstay, you'll be fined and could be detained. You can check your permitted stay with [Migraciones](https://www.gob.pe/migraciones).

**Other nationalities.** Check Bolivia's migration office, [Migración Bolivia](https://www.migracion.gob.bo/), and Peru's [Migraciones](https://www.gob.pe/migraciones). US citizens should start with the [State Department's country pages](https://travel.state.gov/). Bolivia's rules for US visitors have changed recently, so confirm them close to your travel date.

## Altitude: why Cusco first works

This whole route is high. Cusco sits at about 3,400 m ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Cuzco)). Puno is about 3,830 m ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Puno-Peru)), on a lake whose surface is around 3,810 m. La Paz's city centre is about 3,640 m, and the airport at El Alto is above 4,000 m.

Running the route from Cusco towards La Paz means you start slightly lower and climb gradually. Going the other way means landing at El Alto, one of the highest airports you'll ever use, on day one.

Cusco is still a big step if you fly in from sea level. The [NHS advises](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) avoiding flying straight to a high-altitude destination where you can. It also advises against going from below 1,200 m to above 3,500 m in one day. Many travellers spend their first nights in the Sacred Valley, which is lower than Cusco, before sleeping in the city. Machu Picchu is lower again: UNESCO puts it at about 2,430 m ([UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/274/)). Visiting it early in the trip works well with that.

Once you're above 3,000 m, the NHS says to avoid sleeping more than 500 m higher than the night before and to rest every three to four days. For symptoms and when to go down, read our guide to [altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/).

## Machu Picchu tickets

Book Machu Picchu entry through the Peruvian Ministry of Culture's official ticket platform, [TuBoleto](https://tuboleto.cultura.pe/). Peruvian press reports in 2026 said the ministry had tightened the rules: tickets are in the visitor's name, need your passport details, and generally can't be moved to another date or person. Sales are allowed only directly or through registered agencies. Entry numbers are limited and popular dates sell out, so book as soon as your dates are fixed. Bring the passport you booked with.

We don't give prices, as they change. Check the official site, and be wary of resellers you can't verify.

## Route shapes: two, three and four weeks

These are outlines, not fixed itineraries. Each one runs Cusco to La Paz for altitude reasons, and days include travel.

### Two weeks

- **Days 1–4:** Sacred Valley, then Cusco. Take it slowly.
- **Days 5–6:** Machu Picchu.
- **Days 7–8:** To Puno and the Peruvian side of the lake.
- **Days 9–10:** Cross at Kasani to Copacabana and Isla del Sol.
- **Days 11–14:** La Paz, with a day trip, then fly home.

This works. The catch is that it leaves out Uyuni. If the salt flats are a must, look at three weeks, or see our [14-day Bolivia itinerary](/blog/bolivia-14-day-itinerary/) for a Bolivia-only fortnight.

### Three weeks

Follow the two-week plan, then from La Paz head south to Uyuni for the classic [3-day salt flats trip](/blog/uyuni-salt-flats-3-day-tour/). There are domestic flights between La Paz and Uyuni. Check current schedules with the airline, as they change. Finish back in Uyuni, or cross into Chile (below).

Three weeks also leaves room for a spare day, which is worth having given the risk of protests and blockades.

### Four weeks

Add Bolivia's colonial cities. After La Paz, head to Sucre, which is lower at about 2,790 m and a good place to recover. Go on to Potosí, then overland to Uyuni for the salt flats. Our post on [Sucre and Potosí](/blog/sucre-and-potosi/) covers that stretch.

Or, if you'd rather end somewhere warm and low, swap the colonial cities for the Amazon. See [into the Bolivian Amazon from Rurrenabaque](/blog/bolivian-amazon-rurrenabaque/).

## Adding Uyuni, and exiting via Chile

The 3-day salt flats and Lípez trip can end at the Chilean border, with a transfer down to San Pedro de Atacama. That makes it a natural exit if you're flying home from Chile or carrying on south. Ask your operator to confirm the border transfer when you book.

A few things to know:

- **Timing matters.** Whether the salar is a dry white crust or a mirror depends on the season. See [wet season or dry season at Uyuni?](/blog/uyuni-wet-or-dry-season/).
- **Chile has its own rules.** The [FCDO says](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/chile/entry-requirements) British visitors can stay up to 90 days without a visa. It also says Chile has strict rules about goods you can bring in, so check before you pack snacks across the border.
- **Reverse with care.** Starting the trip in San Pedro means a fast climb to the high lagoons on day one. Ending there, after weeks at altitude, is the easier direction.

## Before you book

- **Check entry rules for every passport in your group**, for Bolivia, Peru and Chile if you're going there. Use the official pages, not forum posts.
- **Plan the route from Cusco to La Paz**, starting low in the Sacred Valley, with Machu Picchu early.
- **Book Machu Picchu on the official TuBoleto site** as soon as your dates are fixed.
- **Cross the border in daylight**, get stamped out and stamped in, and keep your entry stamps safe.
- **Leave at least one spare day** for protests, blockades or a slow border queue.
