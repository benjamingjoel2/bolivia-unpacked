The small plane drops out of the clouds and the Andes are suddenly behind you. Below, a brown river loops through unbroken green, and when the door opens the air is thick, warm and loud with insects. A few hours ago you were wearing a down jacket in La Paz. Now you are in the Amazon basin, in a river town where the main traffic is long wooden boats.

Rurrenabaque is Bolivia's most popular gateway to the rainforest and the wetlands beyond it. It is also a completely different trip from the highlands: different health rules, different seasons, different questions to ask before you book. This post covers how to get there, how to choose between the jungle and the pampas, and how to do it well.

## Where Rurrenabaque is

[Rurrenabaque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rurrenabaque) sits on the Beni River in Beni department, in Bolivia's northern lowlands, only a few hundred metres above sea level. Across the river is San Buenaventura, the jumping-off point for Madidi National Park. The town itself is small and relaxed, with tour offices, guesthouses and riverside restaurants. Most people stay a night before and after their trip.

## Getting there from La Paz

You have two options, and the difference is time.

**By air.** Flights from La Paz take about an hour or less. Rurrenabaque's airport was paved in 2010, but low cloud over the nearby mountains can stop planes landing. Reyes airport, about 32 km away, often has better weather. Delays and cancellations happen, especially in the rainy season, so we'd always leave a spare day before an international flight home.

**By road.** The road from La Paz is about 410 km, but the drive is long: buses typically take around 18 hours. It drops from the Altiplano through the Yungas into the lowlands, and conditions vary a lot with weather and roadworks. The UK [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) warns that floods and landslides are common in the rainy season (November to March), that roads can be impassable for days, and that vehicles are often not well maintained. It also notes regular fuel shortages that can disrupt buses.

Many travellers fly at least one way. If you go by road, plan for delays and travel by day where you can.

One more safety note from the same FCDO page: robberies involving taxis have increased in a few cities, Rurrenabaque among them. Ask your guesthouse or operator to arrange transfers.

## Jungle or pampas: two different trips

Most visitors choose between, or combine, two kinds of trip from Rurrenabaque. Both usually last a few days.

### The jungle: Madidi National Park

[Madidi National Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madidi_National_Park), created in 1995 and managed by Bolivia's protected areas service, [SERNAP](https://www.sernap.gob.bo/), covers roughly 19,000 square kilometres and climbs from lowland rainforest to high Andean peaks. It is exceptionally rich in plants and wildlife. Indigenous peoples including the Tacana live in and around it, and several community-run ecolodges operate in and near the park.

A jungle trip usually means a motorised canoe journey upriver to a lodge or camp, then guided walks on forest trails by day and often by night. This is a trip about the forest itself: huge trees, medicinal plants, birdsong, tracks in the mud. Animals are present but often hidden by the canopy, so sightings take patience and luck.

### The pampas: Pampas del Yacuma

The pampas trips head to the wetlands of the Yacuma river. The [Wildlife Conservation Society](https://bolivia.wcs.org/en-us/Landscapes/Madidi-Tambopata/Pampas-del-Yacuma.aspx) describes the area, protected by the municipality of Santa Rosa del Yacuma since 2007, as a mosaic of flooded savannas, wetlands, forest islands and gallery forest shaped by annual flooding. Ecotourism is a major local activity.

Here you spend most of the time in boats on open channels, which makes animals easier to spot than in the forest. WCS lists species of the area including two kinds of caiman, the Bolivian river dolphin and the marsh deer. Birds are a big part of the trip.

### Which to choose

- **Choose the jungle** if you want rainforest, walking and the feeling of being deep inside the forest, and you are happy with fewer but more memorable sightings.
- **Choose the pampas** if you want to see as much wildlife as possible from a boat, with less walking.
- **Do both** if you have the time. Many people do.

## What you might see

Nothing is guaranteed in the wild, and a responsible operator will not promise particular animals. Depending on the trip, the season and luck, you might see caiman, river dolphins, monkeys and a wide variety of birds. Jaguars live in the region, but they are shy and you should not expect to see one. Treat any sighting as a bonus, not something you have paid for.

## Wildlife ethics: look, don't touch

If a trip offers handling animals, swimming after them or chasing them for photos, we'd avoid it. A good guide keeps a respectful distance and does not feed, catch, hold or bait wildlife, and never asks you to.

There are health reasons too. The [CDC](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/bolivia) notes that rabies is found in Bolivia and that rabies vaccine is typically not readily available there after an exposure. Do not touch or feed any animal, wild or domestic, and ask a travel clinic whether rabies vaccination before travel makes sense for you.

## Health: yellow fever and malaria

The lowlands have health risks the highlands do not. Speak to a travel clinic well before you go: the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) suggests checking vaccine recommendations at least eight weeks before your trip.

**Yellow fever vaccine.** The [CDC](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/bolivia) and the UK's [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/countries/bolivia) both recommend vaccination for travellers aged 9 months and over going to areas below 2,300 m east of the Andes, which include the whole of Beni department, where Rurrenabaque is. Neither recommends it for travel only to places above 2,300 m, such as La Paz city.

**Yellow fever certificate on arrival.** Here the official sources are worded differently. The CDC's Bolivia page says proof of vaccination is required for all arriving travellers aged 1 year and over. The UK [FCDO entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/entry-requirements) and TravelHealthPro say a certificate is required if you are arriving from a country with a risk of yellow fever transmission. TravelHealthPro also notes that Bolivia's Ministry of Health advises travellers to risk areas to carry their vaccination card. Check the FCDO and CDC pages, and with the airline and your travel clinic, before you travel. If you are vaccinated, carry the certificate.

**Malaria.** The CDC lists malaria transmission in all areas of Bolivia below 2,500 m (none in La Paz city) and says it recommends prescription medicine to prevent malaria for travellers to certain areas. TravelHealthPro describes the risk in the Amazon basin as low and recommends awareness and bite avoidance, with antimalarial tablets considered only in exceptional circumstances. Because the UK and US advice differ, discuss your exact itinerary with a travel clinic. Either way, avoid bites: long sleeves at dusk, repellent and a net where the lodge provides one. The FCDO also lists Oropouche and Zika among Bolivia's health risks, both of which make bite prevention more important.

## Water safety

Much of the trip is on rivers. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) notes that boats used for river excursions in jungle areas are often very basic, and advises checking that life jackets are provided. Make sure there is one for everyone, in a size that fits, and wear it.

The same page warns that ayahuasca ceremonies are held mainly in the Amazon region, sometimes in remote places where medical help is hard to reach. It is something to avoid if it is offered as an add-on.

## Seasons

The FCDO gives Bolivia's rainy season as November to March, with floods and landslides common and roads sometimes closed for days. In the lowlands that means heavy rain, more flight disruption and higher water. The pampas are shaped by annual flooding, so water levels, and where boats can go, change through the year.

The dry months are generally easier for travel. Whenever you go, ask your operator how the season affects the trip you are booking: which trails are open, how high the rivers are, and how that changes what you might see.

## What to ask an operator

- **Who runs the lodge?** Is it owned or run by a local community, and how does the community benefit?
- **Guides.** Are they local, are they licensed or registered for the protected area, and do they speak a language you share?
- **Park entry.** Are any protected-area entry fees included? Fees change, so check what is covered.
- **Boats.** Are there life jackets for everyone, in the right sizes? Is the boat in good condition?
- **Wildlife policy.** Do guides ever handle, feed or bait animals? (The right answer is no.)
- **Group size and food.** How many people per group and per boat? Can they handle dietary needs?
- **Emergencies.** How do they reach help from the lodge, and how far is the nearest medical care?
- **Weather plans.** What happens if your flight is cancelled or the river is too high?

## Before you book

- **See a travel clinic early** about yellow fever, malaria prevention and rabies, and check the certificate rules on the FCDO and CDC pages before you travel.
- **Leave slack.** Build in a spare day around the La Paz flight or road trip.
- **Choose ethics and safety over price.** A good local operator uses community lodges and licensed guides, puts everyone in a life jacket, and leaves the animals alone.
