The cabin door opens and the air that comes in is cold, dry and thin. You walk down the airbridge at El Alto and notice that you are breathing a little faster than usual, even though you are only carrying a daypack. By the time you reach the baggage belt, there may be a faint pressure behind your eyes. Nothing dramatic. Just your body registering that you have arrived somewhere very high, very quickly.

For most visitors, that is as far as it goes: a few days of headaches, broken sleep and puffing up hills, which ease as the body adjusts. For a smaller number, altitude illness becomes serious. This guide explains what to expect, what the official health guidance says, and how to plan your first days so the altitude becomes part of the experience rather than the thing you remember.

**This is not medical advice.** It is a summary of public health guidance to help you plan. Before you travel, see a GP, travel clinic or travel health professional, especially if you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant, or have had altitude illness before.

## Why La Paz is different

El Alto International Airport sits at about 4,060 m. La Paz itself fills a canyon below the airport, with its centre at roughly 3,600 m and the southern districts lower, at around 3,250 m, according to the [La Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Paz) entry. So you land higher than you will probably sleep, and then you sleep somewhere still well above the height at which altitude illness becomes likely.

[TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/26/altitude-illness) says altitude illness usually occurs above 2,500 m, and advises against going from under 1,200 m to over 3,500 m in a single day. The [CDC Yellow Book chapter on high-altitude travel](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html) notes that destinations which require a rapid ascent by air to above 3,400 m put travellers in a high-risk group for acute mountain sickness. A direct flight from the coast to La Paz is exactly that kind of ascent.

The [FCDO's Bolivia health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) names La Paz and the Salar de Uyuni among the places where altitude sickness is a risk.

## What acute mountain sickness feels like

Acute mountain sickness, or AMS, is the common, milder form of altitude illness. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) lists the main symptoms as:

- a headache
- loss of appetite
- feeling or being sick
- feeling tired or exhausted
- dizziness
- difficulty sleeping

The NHS says symptoms usually start several hours after you arrive. The CDC chapter describes headache as the main symptom, usually with at least one of the others, and compares the feeling to a hangover.

Being out of breath when you walk uphill is normal at this height. Feeling breathless while you are resting is not, and is one of the warning signs below.

## The warning signs that matter

Two less common forms of altitude illness are emergencies: high-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE), which affects the brain, and high-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE), which affects the lungs. TravelHealthPro describes severe AMS, HACE and HAPE as life-threatening conditions that need urgent attention.

Get medical help straight away, and go to a lower altitude if you can, if you or someone with you:

- is confused, very sleepy or difficult to wake
- has problems with balance or coordination, or is stumbling
- is short of breath even when resting
- has a cough, especially one bringing up frothy or bloody spit
- has blue or grey lips, tongue, skin or nails
- is seeing or hearing things that are not real
- feels very unwell, or symptoms are getting worse despite rest

These signs come from the [NHS altitude sickness page](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/), and match the HACE and HAPE descriptions from [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/26/altitude-illness) and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html). In Bolivia, the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) gives 118 as the number for an ambulance. Call your insurer too, as soon as you can.

## What the official guidance says about prevention

The guidance from the NHS, the CDC and TravelHealthPro is consistent on the basics.

**Go up gradually.** Above 3,000 m, the NHS advises trying not to sleep more than 500 m higher than you slept the night before, and taking a rest day at the same altitude every three to four days. TravelHealthPro gives the same figures. What counts is where you sleep, not how high you go during the day.

**Take it easy at first.** The CDC chapter advises limiting yourself to mild exercise and avoiding alcohol for the first 48 hours at high altitude. TravelHealthPro lists exertion on arrival as a risk factor, and says physical fitness does not appear to protect you.

**Drink enough water.** The NHS advises drinking enough so that you do not get dehydrated, and not drinking alcohol while you are travelling or climbing at altitude.

**Do not go higher with symptoms.** The NHS says that if you have symptoms you should rest at the same altitude and not go any higher until they clear. If they do not improve after a day, go down if you can, by about 300 to 1,000 m. The CDC chapter says to descend if symptoms get worse despite rest.

**Medicines are for a doctor to decide.** The NHS says a GP or travel clinic may prescribe medicine to help prevent altitude sickness, for example if you are going up quickly or have had it before. TravelHealthPro and the CDC both discuss acetazolamide, and TravelHealthPro notes it is not suitable for everyone, including in pregnancy. Do not start any medicine for altitude without professional advice, and ask about trying it before you travel.

## What about coca tea?

You will find coca tea, mate de coca, offered in many hotels and cafés in La Paz, and it is part of everyday life in the Andes. As a way to prevent altitude illness, though, the official sources do not back it. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/26/altitude-illness) says evidence for the benefit of coca is either inconclusive or lacking, and does not recommend it to prevent AMS. Neither the NHS page nor the CDC chapter lists it as a prevention measure.

If you enjoy a cup, enjoy it. Just do not treat it as a substitute for a sensible ascent plan.

## Going low before high

The single most useful thing you can do is arrange your trip so you do not start at the top.

If your route includes lower places, put them first. Sucre, at about 2,790 m according to the [Sucre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sucre) entry, is a common stepping stone. Lowland stops such as the Amazon are lower still. Arriving in La Paz after a few days somewhere lower is gentler than arriving cold from sea level. See our [14-day Bolivia itinerary](/blog/bolivia-14-day-itinerary/) for one way to order a route, and [combining Bolivia and Peru in one trip](/blog/bolivia-and-peru-in-one-trip/) if you are coming from Peru.

If you must fly straight into La Paz, sleep as low as practical for the first nights. The CDC chapter notes that for La Paz, travellers can sometimes spend one or two nights lower than the airport before going higher, which may make preventive medicine unnecessary. The southern districts of the city are lower than the centre and much lower than El Alto. A good local operator can suggest where to stay.

After La Paz, keep the same logic for the rest of the trip. Lake Titicaca, at about 3,800 m, is at a similar height, which makes it a natural next stop. The Uyuni trip climbs much higher, so save it until you have acclimatised. See [the Uyuni salt flats: the 3-day tour explained](/blog/uyuni-salt-flats-3-day-tour/).

## Planning your first 48 hours

Here is how we'd shape the first two days after a flight into La Paz. Adjust it to how you feel, and to your doctor's advice.

**Arrival day.** Go straight to your accommodation and rest. Eat lightly, drink water, and skip alcohol. A short, slow walk is fine if you feel well. Do not plan anything else.

**Day one.** Keep it gentle. A slow look around the area near your hotel, with plenty of sitting down. Let the [cable cars](/blog/la-paz-cable-cars/) do the climbing for you rather than walking up the city's steep streets, and take any trip up to El Alto slowly, since it is several hundred metres higher than the centre.

**Day two.** If you feel well, a fuller day of sightseeing at city height. If you still have symptoms, rest again. Do not book anything higher, such as a day trip to a mountain viewpoint, until your symptoms have gone.

A few practical habits help:

- **Pace yourself on stairs and hills.** Walk slower than you think you need to.
- **Watch each other.** People with altitude illness do not always notice their own confusion or clumsiness.
- **Keep your plans flexible.** Book the first nights somewhere comfortable, and leave the first couple of days free.
- **Sleep warm.** Nights in La Paz are cold for much of the year.

## Insurance and higher trips

TravelHealthPro says adequate travel insurance is essential when you travel to altitude. Tell your insurer your planned maximum altitude, and check that the policy covers your activities and helicopter evacuation. That matters if your trip later goes much higher than La Paz, as the Uyuni and Lípez route and mountain trips do.

The FCDO says public healthcare in Bolivia can be inconsistent, and that private clinics usually expect payment upfront. Have access to funds as well as insurance.

## If you only remember three things

- **Ease in.** Go low before high if you can, take the first 48 hours slowly, and avoid alcohol and hard exercise.
- **Never go higher with symptoms.** Rest until they clear. If they get worse, go down and get help.
- **Know the danger signs.** Confusion, clumsiness, breathlessness at rest or a wet cough are emergencies.

And once more: this is not medical advice. See a travel health professional before you go, and follow their guidance over anything you read here, including on this site.
