The boat from Copacabana noses out of the bay and the town falls away behind you. The water is a deep, cold blue. Ahead, Isla del Sol rises out of the lake in long brown terraces, and the light on the water is so bright you squint even in the morning chill.

Lake Titicaca is shared between Peru and Bolivia, and most visitors see it from Puno on the Peruvian side. The Bolivian shore is quieter and, for many travellers, more rewarding: a pilgrimage town, an island threaded with Inca paths, and an easy link to La Paz. Here is how to see it well.

## The lake and its altitude

Titicaca's surface sits at about [3,812 m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Titicaca). The western part of the lake lies in Peru's Puno region and the eastern side in Bolivia's La Paz department. Isla del Sol and Isla de la Luna are both on the Bolivian side.

That height is similar to central La Paz, so if you arrive from the city after a few days there, the lake adds little extra strain. Isla del Sol is higher. The village of Yumani, at the island's southern end, has a weather station listed at about 3,976 m, and the paths climb above the landing stages. The [UK government's health advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) notes that altitude sickness is a risk in parts of Bolivia, and the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) advises not sleeping more than 500 m higher than the night before once you are above 3,000 m. Coming from La Paz, a night on the island stays within that. Coming straight from sea level, it does not, so read [dealing with altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/) first.

## Getting there: La Paz to Copacabana

The drive from La Paz to Copacabana takes roughly a few hours and crosses the open altiplano before reaching the water. Partway along, the road meets the [Strait of Tiquina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Tiquina), the narrow neck of water between San Pablo de Tiquina and San Pedro de Tiquina, under a kilometre across at its narrowest. There is no bridge. Buses and cars go over on wooden barges, and passengers usually cross separately in small boats, then rejoin their vehicle on the far side.

It is a slightly chaotic, very Bolivian interlude. Keep your valuables with you rather than leaving them on the bus. The [UK government](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) also advises using a direct bus between Copacabana and La Paz, and warns that road blockades are used as a form of protest, sometimes without warning. Residents have at times blockaded the Tiquina road themselves, so check the news before you set off.

## Tiwanaku: worth the detour

Tiwanaku is not on the Copacabana road, but it is close enough to La Paz, about 70 km from the city, to visit as a day trip, and some private tours fold it into a longer day on the way to the lake.

It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, listed in 2000 as the spiritual and political centre of the Tiwanaku culture. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/567) describes the city as the capital of a pre-Hispanic empire that reached its height between about AD 500 and 900. You will walk past the terraced Akapana platform, the walled Kalasasaya courtyard with the carved Gate of the Sun, and the Pumapunku platform with its precisely cut stone blocks. Allow time for the site museum. It is high and exposed, so wear a hat and sunscreen and pace yourself.

## Copacabana

Copacabana, at about 3,841 m, is the main town on the Bolivian shore. Its centrepiece is the 17th-century Basilica of Our Lady of Copacabana, a shrine to the patron saint of Bolivia that draws pilgrims from Bolivia and southern Peru. The town sits between Mount Calvario and Mount Niño Calvario. The climb up Calvario at sunset is steep and slow at this height, but the view over the bay is the reward.

Copacabana is also where food on the lake gets interesting. Rainbow trout is an introduced species in Titicaca, and you will see it on lakeside menus. Take your time over lunch: the town is built for slow afternoons.

## The boat to Isla del Sol

Boats to Isla del Sol leave from Copacabana's waterfront. Schedules and landing points change with the season and with local arrangements, so check the day before. The [UK government](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) notes that boats on Lake Titicaca are often very basic and advises checking that life jackets are provided. Do that before you board, not halfway across.

## Walking the island

Isla del Sol is not a place to rush. Its hillsides are covered in agricultural terraces, and Yumani and Challapampa are the largest of several villages, home to families who live mainly by farming, with fishing and tourism on the side.

In the south, near Yumani, an Inca stairway climbs from the shore past a fountain, and the ruins of Pillkukayna stand at the southern end. In the north are the labyrinth-like Chinkana and the sandstone outcrop of Titi Qala, the sacred rock from which, according to legend, the first Inca, Manco Cápac, emerged. The trail between north and south runs along the island's spine with the lake on both sides.

Walk slowly. Even well-acclimatised travellers feel the stairs. Carry water, sun protection and a warm layer.

## Respecting island communities, and checking access

The island is home to working communities, not an open-air museum. Paths cross farmland. If a community charges a visitor fee, pay it, keep to the trails, and ask before photographing people.

Access has been disrupted in the past. In 2017, a [dispute between the communities of Challa and Challapampa](https://eju.tv/2017/07/dos-comunidades-en-pugna-por-turismo-toman-la-isla-del-sol/) over tourism led to the north of the island being closed to visitors, with only the southern port of Yumani open, and boats carrying tourists were turned back. More recent travel reports suggest the north has reopened, but we could not find an official statement. Check current access with your operator or in Copacabana before you go, and plan the walk accordingly.

## Isla de la Luna

[Isla de la Luna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isla_de_la_Luna) lies east of Isla del Sol and is much smaller, at about 1 km². Ruins of an Inca building, described as a nunnery, stand on its eastern shore. Fewer people visit, which is part of its appeal. Some boats combine the two islands in a day. Ask in Copacabana what is running.

## Weather and cold nights

The altiplano has dry winters with very cold nights and mornings, and most rain falls in summer thunderstorms. Even on a bright day, the sun at this height is fierce and the wind off the water is cold. Simple island guesthouses may have little or no heating, so pack a proper warm layer, a hat and gloves, and treat a night on the island as a night in the mountains.

## Linking to Puno and Peru

The lake is the usual overland link between La Paz and Puno, and on to Cusco. The road border near Copacabana is at Kasani and Yunguyo, where Peru's migration authority [runs a control post](https://caretas.pe/nacional/migraciones-fortalece-el-control-migratorio-de-puno-frontera-con-bolivia). The other main crossing is at Desaguadero.

The [UK government's entry advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/entry-requirements) says to make sure your passport is stamped on both sides of a land border, and to get an entry stamp when you arrive in Bolivia, or you may be fined when you leave. The UK government also warns that foreign visitors are vulnerable to express kidnapping at overland border crossings, including with Peru, so use reputable transport. Puno is the lake's largest town and its main Peruvian port, and the Uros floating islands sit near it.

## Before you go

- **Arrive acclimatised.** Spend a few days in La Paz or elsewhere at altitude before sleeping on Isla del Sol.
- **Check island access and boat times the day before.** Arrangements change, and the north of the island has been closed before.
- **Pack for cold.** Warm layers, sun protection and a torch for evening paths.
- **Check life jackets on every boat.** Basic boats are the norm here.
