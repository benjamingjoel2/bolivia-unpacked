The jeep leaves Uyuni in the morning with the roof rack strapped tight: rucksacks, a gas bottle, jerrycans, a sack of food for three days. Within minutes the town gives way to flat, pale scrub and a line of rusting locomotives. An hour or so later the tyres hiss onto the salt, the horizon goes white in every direction, and the only sense of scale is a far-off volcano floating above the heat haze.

That first hour is the reason most people come. But the classic 3-day trip is much more than the salt flat. It runs south through Sur Lípez, a high, empty corner of Bolivia of coloured lagoons, flamingos, geysers and volcanoes, and it sleeps you higher than most travellers have ever slept. This is how the trip usually works, day by day, and what to sort out before you book.

## How the trip works

The standard format is three days and two nights in a 4x4 with a driver, who usually also cooks or travels with a cook. Several jeeps often travel loosely together. You sleep in basic lodges in small villages, normally in shared rooms.

Most trips start and finish in the town of Uyuni. A common variation ends on the third morning at the Chilean border, where you transfer onward to San Pedro de Atacama; the same trip can run in reverse from Chile. Longer versions start or end in Tupiza. One-day trips cover only the salar itself.

The order of stops below is the usual one in the dry season. In the wet season, water on the salt can change it completely; see [wet season or dry season at Uyuni?](/blog/uyuni-wet-or-dry-season/).

## Day 1: the train cemetery and the salt flat

The first stop is the train cemetery, about 3 km outside Uyuni, where engines and wagons from the old railway lines were left to rust after the mining industry declined in the 1940s. It is a strange, photogenic place and usually busy, because every jeep stops here first.

Then comes the [Salar de Uyuni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salar_de_Uyuni) itself, the largest salt flat in the world. [NASA Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-salt-bath-in-bolivia-149502/) puts its salt crust at about 10,000 square kilometres, and it lies at about 3,656 m. In the dry season the surface is white and broken into ridged polygons that stretch to the horizon. Drivers stop in the middle for the perspective photos everyone wants, where a friend appears to stand on a water bottle.

Lunch is often near or on Isla Incahuasi, an outcrop in the middle of the salar, the remnant of an old volcano, covered in giant cacti. A path climbs to a viewpoint over the salt. When the salar is flooded, many jeeps cannot reach it, and the day is rearranged.

You sleep the first night on or near the southern edge of the salar, at roughly the height of the salt flat itself.

## Day 2: lagoons, the Stone Tree and Laguna Colorada

The second day is long and rough. The track leaves the salt and climbs into Sur Lípez, past volcanoes and a chain of small high lagoons where flamingos feed in the shallows. The [Eduardo Avaroa Andean Fauna National Reserve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduardo_Avaroa_Andean_Fauna_National_Reserve), run by [SERNAP](https://www.sernap.gob.bo/), covers much of this route, at heights of about 4,200 m to 5,400 m. Three species of flamingo live on its lakes: Chilean, Andean and James's.

In the afternoon you cross the sand and rock of the Siloli area to the Árbol de Piedra, the Stone Tree, a wind-carved rock that balances on a narrow stem. Then comes Laguna Colorada, at about 4,278 m, a very shallow lake that turns red and pink from algae, edged with white mineral deposits and dotted with flamingos.

The reserve charges visitors an entrance fee, which goes towards conservation. The amount changes, so ask your operator whether it is included in the price or paid in cash on the day.

The second night is usually spent in a lodge near Laguna Colorada, inside the reserve and above about 4,200 m. For most people it is the coldest and highest night of their trip so far, and often a poor night's sleep.

## Day 3: geysers, hot springs and Laguna Verde

Day three starts in the dark. Drivers aim to reach the [Sol de Mañana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sol_de_Ma%C3%B1ana) geothermal field early, when the steam shows best against the cold air. At around 4,900 m it is normally the highest point of the trip: a field of steam vents, boiling mud pools and fumaroles that smell of sulphur. Stay on firm ground, keep well back from the pools, and do not lean over the vents.

Most jeeps then stop at hot springs for a warm soak as the sun comes up, before driving to Laguna Verde, at about 4,310 m, close to the Chilean border at the foot of Licancabur volcano. Its colour, from turquoise to dark green, depends on minerals in the water and how much the wind stirs it.

From here, travellers going to Chile are dropped at the border to meet a transfer down to San Pedro de Atacama. Everyone else faces a long drive back north to Uyuni, usually arriving in the late afternoon or evening.

## Altitude: how high you sleep

This is a fast climb. You go from the salar at about 3,656 m to nights above 4,200 m and a morning at around 4,900 m, in two days. The [UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) names the Salar de Uyuni as one of the places in Bolivia where altitude sickness is a risk.

The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) advises not travelling from below 1,200 m to above 3,500 m in one day, and taking a rest day every three to four days once you are at 3,000 m or more. Flying into Uyuni from near sea level and setting off the next morning breaks that advice. Starting from San Pedro de Atacama, which is much lower, is a faster climb still.

In practice, we'd spend at least a few days at altitude first, in La Paz, Potosí or Sucre, before the trip. Our guide to [dealing with altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/) covers the early days.

Headache, nausea, dizziness and poor sleep are common symptoms. The NHS advises getting medical help immediately for confusion, problems with balance, breathlessness at rest, or a cough with frothy or bloody spit, and going down straight away if you can. On this trip, "down" may be hours away, which is why the operator's plan matters. Ask a GP or travel clinic before you go whether medicine to help prevent altitude sickness is right for you.

## The cold

Nights are cold all year here. Long-term averages for Uyuni give night lows from about −9 °C to 5 °C depending on the month, and the lodges in Sur Lípez are higher and colder still. Lodges are basic and many have no heating; ask what to expect for hot water and electricity.

Bring:

- a warm down jacket, hat, gloves and thermal base layers you can sleep in;
- a sleeping bag rated for well below freezing, if the operator does not supply one (and check what they supply);
- sunglasses, high-factor sun cream and lip balm: the sun on the salt is fierce;
- a head torch, in case power in the lodge is limited;
- cash in small notes, snacks, a refillable water bottle and a basic first-aid kit;
- a swimsuit and small towel for the hot springs.

## Safety: what the official advice says

The UK government's [safety and security advice for Bolivia](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) lists salt flat tours among the country's adventure activities and states that there are no official minimum standards for tour operators. It advises getting local advice, using only reputable companies, and checking that your insurance covers what you plan to do.

The same page says serious accidents can happen on the main tourist routes and that vehicles are often not well maintained. It also warns of regular petrol and diesel shortages across the country, and of floods and road closures in the rainy season (November to March), when travellers can be stranded in remote high areas. It advises carrying food, water and warm clothing in case of delays.

Put plainly: the jeep and the driver matter more than anything else on this trip. Choose your operator on safety, not price.

## What to ask an operator

Before you book, ask:

- **Vehicles.** How old is the jeep, when was it last serviced, and do the seatbelts work in every seat? Is there a spare tyre and tools?
- **Drivers.** How long do drivers drive each day, do they get proper rest between trips, and is alcohol banned on tour?
- **Fuel.** Does each vehicle carry spare fuel, given the shortages?
- **Oxygen and first aid.** Is there oxygen and a first-aid kit in every vehicle, and does the driver know how to use them?
- **Communication.** How does the driver call for help where there is no mobile signal?
- **Group size.** How many passengers share each jeep, and is everyone in a proper seat?
- **Altitude plan.** What happens if someone gets ill at the Laguna Colorada lodge? Can they be driven down?
- **Inclusions.** Are sleeping bags, the reserve entrance fee and the border transfer to Chile included?

A good local operator will answer all of this without hesitation.

## Before you book

- **Arrive acclimatised.** Spend a few days at altitude before day one; don't start the morning after flying in from low ground.
- **Pick the jeep, not the price.** Ask the questions above and get the answers in writing.
- **Pack for nights below freezing.** Warm layers, a proper sleeping bag, sun protection and a head torch.
- **Check the season.** The route, and whether you can reach Isla Incahuasi, depend on water on the salar. See [wet season or dry season at Uyuni?](/blog/uyuni-wet-or-dry-season/) and, for fitting the trip into a wider route, our [14-day Bolivia itinerary](/blog/bolivia-14-day-itinerary/).
