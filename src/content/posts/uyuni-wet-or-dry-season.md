Picture the same spot twice. In September the jeep stops in the middle of a white plain so bright you squint through sunglasses, the salt underfoot crunching into ridged, many-sided tiles that run to the horizon. In February, at the same spot, the driver cuts the engine in a thin film of water, the clouds lie on the ground as well as in the sky, and you can no longer tell where the salt ends and the air begins.

Both are the Salar de Uyuni. Which one you see depends almost entirely on when you go, and the choice changes more than the photos. It changes the route, the comfort and what can go wrong. This post lays out the trade-offs so you can pick your months with your eyes open.

## Two seasons, one salt flat

Bolivia's year is shaped less by temperature than by rain. On the Altiplano around Uyuni there is one wet season and one long dry one.

The [UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) gives Bolivia's rainy season as November to March. Climate records for Uyuni summarised on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salar_de_Uyuni) show rain is low from April to November, with January the wettest month, and describe the salar turning into a shallow lake at times between December and April. Those dates overlap rather than match, which is honest: the edges of the season move from year to year. For current conditions, Bolivia's weather service, [SENAMHI](https://senamhi.gob.bo/), publishes forecasts and warnings.

## The mirror: what it is and when it happens

The mirror is rainwater. When rain falls on a salt flat this level, a thin sheet of water can sit on the surface, and on a calm day it reflects the sky.

[NASA Earth Observatory](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/a-salt-bath-in-bolivia-149502/) describes the salar taking on a "mirror-like appearance" in the rainy season, while for much of the year it is an expanse of white. Its images from early 2022 show what a big year looks like: after heavy rain in November, December and early January, the salar was brimming with water nearly to its edges. NASA linked that above-normal filling to a strong La Niña.

What that means for planning:

- **The mirror belongs to the wet season**, roughly late December to March, and sometimes into April after a wet year.
- **It is never guaranteed.** A dry rainy season may leave little standing water. Too much water brings its own problems (see below).
- **Wind spoils it.** Ripples break the reflection, so calm, clear spells are what you want.
- **It is patchy.** Water collects in some areas and not others. Drivers usually know where the shallow, reflective patches are on a given day.

Salty water is hard on shoes, clothes and camera gear. Ask your operator whether rubber boots are provided in the wet season, and bring a cloth to wipe salt off your camera.

## The hexagons: the dry-season salt

From roughly May to October the salt is dry, hard and blinding white. In many areas the crust breaks into the low, ridged, many-sided tiles that appear in so many photos, often called the hexagons, stretching in every direction. Photos of these [hexagonal formations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salar_de_Uyuni) are typically from the dry season.

The dry season has the obvious advantages:

- the full route is normally open, from the salar to Isla Incahuasi and south through Sur Lípez to the lagoons and geysers;
- skies are often clear, so days are bright and the stars at night are spectacular;
- the perspective photos work best on the flat, dry white surface.

The trade-off is the cold, which we come to below.

## What water closes or changes in the wet season

The same water that makes the mirror can block the trip.

**Crossing the salar.** NASA Earth Observatory notes that in early 2022 local newspapers reported flooding and temporary bans on travel across the salar during the busy tourist season. In a very wet year, parts of the flat may simply be off limits.

**Isla Incahuasi.** The island sits in the middle of the salar. When the water is deep, jeeps often cannot reach it, and lunch and the viewpoint drop out of the plan.

**The route south.** The standard 3-day trip normally crosses the salar and heads south into Sur Lípez. With water on the salt, operators may go around the edge instead, change the order of stops, or swap the salar day for a shorter mirror trip near the edge. Ask how they handle it before you book. Our [3-day tour explained](/blog/uyuni-salt-flats-3-day-tour/) covers the usual dry-season route.

**Roads more widely.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) warns that floods and landslides are common in the rainy season, that roads can become impassable for days at a time, that four-wheel drive is often needed, and that travellers can be stranded in remote high areas. It advises carrying food, water and warm clothing in case of delays.

**Weather.** Cloudy and stormy days are more common in the wet months, so you can arrive at the salar to grey skies and no reflection at all.

## The dry winter: cold, cold nights

Days on the Altiplano can be pleasant all year. Average daytime highs at Uyuni peak at about 21 °C from November to January and fall to about 13 °C in June, according to the climate records on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salar_de_Uyuni). Nights are cold in every month, with average lows ranging from about −9 °C to 5 °C over the year.

The coldest nights fall in the southern winter, roughly June to August. On the 3-day trip you sleep higher than Uyuni, in basic, often unheated lodges in Sur Lípez, so expect nights well below freezing. With the right sleeping bag and layers it is manageable; without them it is miserable.

Altitude is a factor in every season. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) names the Salar de Uyuni among the places in Bolivia where altitude sickness is a risk, and the cold can make poor sleep at height worse. See [dealing with altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/) for how to acclimatise before you go.

## The shoulder months

The months at the edges of the rains can be a good compromise, with caveats.

- **April.** Often drying out. After a wet year, some shallow water can linger in places, giving a chance of reflections while the route south is usually open again. After a dry year, there may be none.
- **November and early December.** Usually still dry on the salar, with the full route normally open and nights a little milder than in winter. The FCDO counts November as part of the rainy season, so early storms are possible; the mirror is unlikely this early.

Neither is a sure thing. They are for travellers who want a bit of both and accept they may get only one.

## How to decide

Start with what matters most to you.

**Choose the wet season (roughly late December to March) if:**

- the mirror is the photo you have travelled for;
- you are happy for the route to change, possibly losing Isla Incahuasi or some of the lagoons;
- you have flexible days in case of road closures.

**Choose the dry season (roughly May to October) if:**

- you want the full 3-day trip to the lagoons, geysers and Laguna Verde, as planned;
- you prefer clear skies and the classic white, hexagon-patterned salt;
- you can cope with very cold nights, especially from June to August.

**Consider the shoulder months (April, November) if** you want the full route with milder conditions and a slim chance of water, and you are relaxed about which you get.

If Uyuni is one stop in a longer trip, the rest of the route matters too. The wet season brings road disruption elsewhere in Bolivia as well. ## If you only remember three things

- **The mirror needs rain, and rain is unpredictable.** Late December to March is your best chance, never a promise.
- **Water changes the route.** In a wet year, Isla Incahuasi and the crossing south may be off; ask your operator how they adapt.
- **The dry season is cold.** The full route is normally open from May to October, but pack for nights well below freezing, especially in June, July and August.
