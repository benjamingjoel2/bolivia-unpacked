Late afternoon in Sucre, and the sun has turned the walls of the old centre almost too bright to look at. You'll find yourself on the main square with a coffee, watching students cross between whitewashed buildings and red-tiled roofs, and notice something you haven't felt for days: you can climb a flight of stairs without stopping halfway.

A few hours' drive away, and well over a kilometre higher, Potosí feels like another world. The air is thin and cold, the streets are steep, and above the rooftops sits Cerro Rico, the mountain whose silver once paid for an empire and cost countless lives.

Together the two cities are Bolivia's colonial heart. Both are on the UNESCO World Heritage List, and they fit neatly into most routes between La Paz and the [Uyuni salt flats](/blog/uyuni-salt-flats-3-day-tour/). Here's how to see them well.

## Sucre: the white city

Sucre is Bolivia's constitutional capital and home to its Supreme Court, while the government sits in La Paz, according to [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sucre). The Spanish founded it in the late 1530s as La Plata (UNESCO gives 1538, Britannica 1539), and it was later renamed in honour of Antonio José de Sucre, a leader of the independence struggle.

The [Historic City of Sucre](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/566/) was added to the World Heritage List in 1991. UNESCO highlights its 16th-century religious buildings, which mix local building traditions with styles brought from Europe. The many white-walled buildings are why it is often called the "white city".

What to look out for:

- **Casa de la Libertad.** Built in the 17th century as part of a Jesuit complex, this is where the events that led to Bolivia's independence took place. Britannica notes that a congress declared the country independent on 6 August 1825, and the declaration is on display here.
- **The old centre on foot.** Sucre rewards slow wandering: churches, cloisters, courtyards and rooftop views. Many visitors simply spend a day walking without a plan.
- **Dinosaur tracks at Cal Orck'o**, on the edge of the city, and **weaving villages** such as Tarabuco nearby, known for their textiles. Ask locally about how to visit, as arrangements change.

### A gentle place to acclimatise

Sucre sits in a valley at about 2,790 m ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Sucre)). That is still high. The [NHS says](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) altitude sickness can happen above about 2,500 m. But it is much lower than La Paz or Potosí, which makes Sucre a good first stop or a place to recover.

If you're heading up to Potosí and Uyuni, we'd spend at least two or three nights in Sucre first. NHS guidance is to avoid sleeping more than 500 m higher than the night before once you're above 3,000 m, and to take a rest day every three to four days. Going straight from Sucre to Potosí is a bigger jump than that, so give yourself an easy first day there. Our guide to [altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/) covers symptoms and when to go down.

## Potosí: the silver city

Potosí stands at about 4,090 m, according to [Canada's official travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/bolivia), which makes it higher than La Paz and one of the highest cities in the world. It grew from nothing after silver was found in the mountain above it in 1545.

The [City of Potosí](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/420/) was inscribed by UNESCO in 1987. UNESCO describes Cerro Rico as the world's largest silver deposit and notes that the whole production chain survives: the mines, the reservoirs that supplied water power, the mills that ground the ore, and the Royal Mint where silver was turned into bars and coins.

### The silver history, told honestly

It is easy to tell Potosí's story as one of fabulous wealth. The other half matters as much.

The mines ran on forced labour. Under the colonial *mita*, Indigenous communities across the Andes had to send men to work in turn for the Spanish. UNESCO notes that by the 17th century around 13,500 Indigenous people were compelled to work in the mines under this system. [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/repartimiento) explains that the legal limits on this labour were largely ignored. It also records that enslaved Africans were brought in to work as well.

Mercury was used to separate silver from the ore, and that brought its own dangers. Silver from Potosí flowed out to Spain and beyond. The Indigenous communities who dug it out of the mountain paid the price.

Mining has never stopped. In 2014 UNESCO added the city to its [List of World Heritage in Danger](https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/1148), citing uncontrolled mining, the risk that it could damage the historic site, and the instability of Cerro Rico itself.

### Casa Nacional de Moneda

If you see one thing in Potosí, make it the mint. [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/place/Casa-de-la-Moneda) says the first mint was built in the 1570s and rebuilt in the 18th century. It served as the Royal Mint for Spain's American colonies and is now a museum of local history, with mining machinery, ethnography and art. Check opening times and visiting arrangements locally, as they can change.

After the mint, wander the colonial streets around the main square, part of the same World Heritage listing. Walk slowly: at this height, a gentle uphill street is hard work.

## Mine tours: think before you go

Tours into Cerro Rico's working mines are one of the best-known things to do in Potosí. They are not a museum visit. They take you into working mines that are still being dug today, in tunnels that are often hot, cramped and dusty, at an altitude where every step costs you breath.

There is no official guidance saying these tours are safe, and we won't suggest they are. The UK FCDO's [safety and security page for Bolivia](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) doesn't mention the mines at all. [Canada's travel advice](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/bolivia) lists Potosí mine tours among the adventure activities you should only do with well-established companies. It also warns that Bolivia has no official minimum safety standards for tour operators. UNESCO's warnings about the instability of Cerro Rico are worth remembering too.

If you're considering a tour, weigh these points:

- **Your health.** Think hard if you have heart or breathing problems, are not yet used to the altitude, or dislike confined spaces. A tour is no place to discover you're claustrophobic.
- **Your insurance.** Check that your policy covers it. Many policies exclude some adventure activities.
- **The operator.** Ask what safety gear they provide (helmet, lamp, boots, protective clothing, a mask against dust), how their guides are trained, how many people go in each group, and what happens if someone feels unwell underground.
- **The ethics.** You are walking into someone's workplace, often a dangerous one. Ask how the tour benefits the miners and their families. Follow your guide's lead on photos and on gifts. Many visitors decide the mint and the town tell the story well enough, and that is a perfectly good choice.

## Food you might try

Both cities are good places to eat your way through Bolivian classics.

- **Salteñas.** Bolivia's much-loved baked pastry, with a slightly sweet crust around a juicy, stew-like filling of meat or chicken, potato and a little chilli. They're usually a mid-morning snack, and many vendors sell out before lunch. Sucre is one of several cities that claim the best version, and Potosí is also linked to the dish's early history. Eat them upright and bite carefully: the filling runs.
- **Markets.** The central markets in both cities are good for fruit juices, soups and set lunches. Look for busy stalls with high turnover.
- **Potatoes in many forms.** Potatoes are an Andean staple, so you'll meet them in soups and stews at almost every lunch. On a cold Potosí evening, a bowl of soup is hard to beat.

Prices change and we don't list them. Ask at your hotel or look at the menu boards, which usually show the day's offer.

## Getting between the two

Sucre and Potosí are a few hours apart by road, and buses and shared taxis run between them. Sucre also has an airport with domestic flights, so it is an easy place to join or leave a route. Potosí is a common road link onward to Uyuni.

Two practical cautions from the [UK FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security). Road travel in Bolivia can be dangerous because of poor roads and low driving standards. Protest groups also use road blockades, often without warning. Travel by day where you can, choose reputable transport, and leave slack in your plans in case a road closes.

## If you only remember three things

1. **Go low before high.** Spend your first nights in Sucre (about 2,790 m) before Potosí (about 4,090 m), and take your first day in Potosí easy.
2. **See the mint, and think hard about the mine.** The Casa Nacional de Moneda tells the silver story well. A mine tour is a real risk with no official safety standards behind it. If you go, choose a well-established operator, check your insurance and know your limits.
3. **Remember the people behind the silver.** Potosí's wealth was built on forced Indigenous labour. Knowing that makes the city more moving, not less worth visiting.
