The plane drops through thin, bright air and lands on a plateau that seems to have no edge. You step out at El Alto, and the first breath feels slightly short, as if someone has quietly turned down the oxygen. Then the taxi tips over the rim of the canyon and La Paz opens up below you: red-brick houses pouring down the slopes, and the snow-covered peaks of Illimani standing over the far side of the city.

That first view tells you most of what you need to know about Bolivia. It is high, it is dramatic, and it rewards travellers who plan around its geography rather than against it. This guide is the place to start.

## How the country fits together

Bolivia is easiest to understand as three broad zones stacked from west to east.

**The Altiplano and the high Andes.** In the west, two ranges of the Andes enclose a high plateau, the Altiplano. Lake Titicaca sits on it at about 3,800 m, and much of the plateau is higher still, according to the [geography of Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Bolivia). This is where you find La Paz, the Salar de Uyuni and the lagoons and volcanoes of the far south-west. It is also where most first trips spend most of their time.

**The valleys and the Yungas.** On the eastern side of the Andes, the land falls away through deep valleys and the steep, cloud-forested slopes known as the Yungas. Sucre sits in this middle band at about 2,790 m, noticeably lower and milder than La Paz.

**The lowlands.** The eastern plains, from the Amazon basin in the north to the dry Chaco in the south, cover more than two-thirds of the country. Rurrenabaque, the gateway to the Bolivian Amazon, sits less than 300 m above sea level.

La Paz is the seat of government, with the presidential palace and the legislature. Sucre is the constitutional capital and home of the judiciary, as the [La Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Paz) entry explains. The city of La Paz fills a canyon whose centre is at roughly 3,600 m; El Alto, a separate city, sits on the plateau above it, and its airport is at about 4,060 m.

## Entry basics

Entry rules depend on your passport, and Bolivia's rules have changed more than once in recent years. Treat what follows as a starting point and check again close to your travel date.

- **UK citizens.** The [UK government's entry requirements page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/entry-requirements) says you can visit Bolivia for up to 90 days without a visa, with 90 days of visa-free travel allowed in a one-year period. Border officials usually stamp 30 days. Your passport should be valid for at least six months after the date you arrive and have at least two blank pages. You may be asked to show a booking, with its address, for at least your first night.
- **US citizens.** Bolivia dropped its tourist visa requirement for US citizens from 1 December 2025, allowing stays of up to 90 days, according to an [Associated Press report](https://www.ksat.com/news/world/2025/12/01/bolivia-eases-visa-requirements-for-us-and-israeli-travelers/). This rule has been reversed before, so confirm it on the [US State Department's Bolivia page](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/international-travel/International-Travel-Country-Information-Pages/Bolivia.html) before you travel.
- **Everyone else.** Check Bolivia's migration office, the [Dirección General de Migración](https://www.migracion.gob.bo/), and your own government's travel advice.

Whatever your passport, make sure you get an entry stamp. The FCDO warns that without one you may be fined when you leave, and that at land borders you should get stamped on both sides.

## Health: altitude first, then the lowlands

**Altitude is the big one.** The [FCDO health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) names La Paz and the Salar de Uyuni among the places where altitude sickness is a risk. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/factsheet/26/altitude-illness) says altitude illness usually occurs above 2,500 m, and advises avoiding going from under 1,200 m to over 3,500 m in a single day. Flying straight into La Paz does exactly that. Our full guide to [dealing with altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/) covers symptoms, warning signs and how to plan your first two days.

**Yellow fever and malaria are lowland issues.** The [CDC's Bolivia page](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/bolivia) recommends yellow fever vaccine for travellers aged 9 months and over going to areas below 2,300 m east of the Andes, and does not recommend it for travel limited to higher places such as the cities of La Paz and Sucre. It lists malaria in areas below 2,500 m, with no transmission in La Paz itself. If your route includes the Amazon or other lowlands, see a travel health professional well before you go.

**Certificates on arrival.** The UK says you need proof of yellow fever vaccination if you arrive from a country listed as a transmission risk. The CDC page words the requirement more broadly. Because the two sources differ, check both, and ask your travel clinic, before you travel.

**Healthcare and insurance.** The FCDO says public healthcare can be inconsistent, that private clinics usually expect payment upfront, and that the ambulance number is 118. TravelHealthPro says insurance for altitude should cover your planned maximum altitude and helicopter evacuation. Tell your insurer how high you are going.

## When to go

The FCDO describes the rainy season as running from November to March, when landslides can block roads. The rest of the year is drier. In the highlands that usually means clear skies and cold nights, while the lowlands stay warm all year.

Neither season is simply better. The wet months can bring the famous mirror effect to the salt flats, but also road and flight disruption, especially in the lowlands. The drier months suit trekking and the full overland route through the south-west. We weigh it up in detail in [wet season or dry season at Uyuni?](/blog/uyuni-wet-or-dry-season/)

## Getting around

Distances are long and the roads climb over high passes, so most trips mix domestic flights with time on the road.

- **Flights** save whole days, for example between La Paz and Uyuni, or from La Paz down to Rurrenabaque. Check current routes with the airlines, as they change.
- **Buses** link almost every town, but the [FCDO's safety page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) warns that road travel can be dangerous, that long-distance bus accidents are common and that seatbelts are rare. It notes that tourist-oriented companies tend to have better safety records.
- **4x4 tours** are the usual way to cross the Salar de Uyuni and the lagoons beyond it. See [the Uyuni salt flats: the 3-day tour explained](/blog/uyuni-salt-flats-3-day-tour/).
- **In La Paz**, the cable car network links the city with El Alto. It is one of the best ways to see the city, as we explain in [riding La Paz's cable cars](/blog/la-paz-cable-cars/).

## Money and staying connected

We do not give prices on this site, because they change quickly and a figure that was right last season can mislead you this one. A few general habits help:

- Carry some cash in local currency for markets, small shops and rural areas, and keep it spread between pockets and bags.
- Tell your bank you are travelling, and carry more than one way to pay.
- Expect mobile signal and internet to be good in the cities and patchy or absent in remote areas such as the far south-west and deep in the Amazon. Download maps and anything you need offline before you set off.
- Ask your operator what is included and what you will need to pay locally, such as park entry, so there are no surprises.

## Staying safe

Most visits to Bolivia go smoothly, but the country has some particular risks, and the [FCDO's travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia) is worth reading in full before you go.

**Protests and road blockades.** The FCDO says protests can occur with little notice and blockades can start without warning, including on roads to and from airports. Its latest update, from September 2026, concerns a State of Emergency. It advises against trying to cross a blockade, building in extra travel time and being ready to change plans. A spare day or two in your itinerary is the simplest protection.

**Fuel.** The FCDO's safety page mentions regular petrol and diesel shortages, which can strand drivers and reduce bus services.

**Crime.** The FCDO warns about petty theft in central La Paz, about thieves posing as police, and about express kidnappings, which mostly affect tourists travelling alone. It advises using established or radio taxi companies rather than hailing cars on the street.

**Areas to avoid.** At the time of writing, the FCDO advises against all but essential travel to the Chapare region of Cochabamba department. It is not on standard first-trip routes, but check the current advice in case this changes.

## How long to stay

Bolivia rewards time. Because you need to acclimatise and the distances are long, a very short trip spends a large share of its days recovering or in transit.

As a rough guide:

- **About a week** lets you see La Paz and Lake Titicaca, or La Paz and the salt flats, at a sensible pace.
- **Around two weeks** is enough for a classic loop through La Paz, Lake Titicaca, Sucre and Potosí, and Uyuni. Our [14-day Bolivia itinerary](/blog/bolivia-14-day-itinerary/) lays it out.
- **Longer** lets you add the Amazon from Rurrenabaque, the Yungas, or a combination with Peru. See [Lake Titicaca from the Bolivian side](/blog/lake-titicaca-bolivian-side/), [Sucre and Potosí](/blog/sucre-and-potosi/), [into the Bolivian Amazon](/blog/bolivian-amazon-rurrenabaque/) and [combining Bolivia and Peru in one trip](/blog/bolivia-and-peru-in-one-trip/).

Whatever the length, we'd build in at least one spare day for weather, blockades or simply feeling the altitude.

## Why a local operator helps

You can travel Bolivia independently, and many people do. But this is a country where the order of a route matters, where a closed road can rewrite your week, and where the FCDO notes there are no official minimum standards for adventure tour companies.

A good local operator earns its keep in a few specific ways:

- **Route order.** Arranging your nights so you sleep lower before you sleep higher, for example starting in Sucre or the lowlands before La Paz.
- **Disruption.** Knowing which roads are blocked this week and rerouting you, rather than leaving you to work it out at a bus terminal.
- **Vehicles and drivers.** On the salt flats and in the south-west, the quality of the vehicle and the driver matters more than anything else.
- **Local knowledge.** Advice on safe taxis, the best time to visit a market, and what has changed since the guidebooks were written.

Ask any operator about its safety practices, its drivers' experience and what happens if someone becomes unwell at altitude.

## Before you book

- Check entry rules for your passport on official pages, and check again a week or two before you fly.
- See a travel health professional about altitude and, if you are going to the lowlands, yellow fever and malaria.
- Make sure your insurance covers your highest planned altitude and evacuation.
- Plan a route that eases into altitude, with easy first days in La Paz.
- Leave at least one spare day for blockades and weather.
- Read the latest [FCDO travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia) shortly before you leave.
