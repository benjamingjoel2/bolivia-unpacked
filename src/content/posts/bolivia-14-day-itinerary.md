The jeep pulls away from Uyuni while the light is still thin and grey, and within minutes the town has gone and there is nothing ahead but white. By mid-morning you are standing on a hump of cactus-covered rock in the middle of the salt, looking out over a flat that seems to have no edge.

That moment is why most people come to Bolivia. The trick is getting there, and to La Paz and Lake Titicaca afterwards, without spending half your trip in bed with a pounding head. This two-week route is built around one idea: arrive low, then climb in steps.

## Why the order matters

Most of the places travellers want to see in Bolivia sit well above 3,000 m. The [CDC Yellow Book](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html), drawing on Wilderness Medical Society guidance, advises that once you are above 3,000 m you should raise your sleeping altitude by no more than 500 m a day, and plan an extra day of acclimatisation for every further 1,000 m. It also notes that the WMS recommends against going straight to a sleeping altitude of 2,750 m or more in a single day. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) gives similar advice, adds a rest day every three to four days once you are at 3,000 m or more, and says to avoid going from below 1,200 m to above 3,500 m in one day.

Flying straight into La Paz breaks that rule by design. The [UK government's health advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) names La Paz and the Salar de Uyuni as places where altitude sickness is a risk. So this route starts in the lowlands and finishes in La Paz, by which point you have been living at altitude for more than a week.

One honest caveat: no road route in Bolivia fits the 500 m rule perfectly. The jump from Sucre up to Potosí is bigger than that, and we say so below. The plan softens it with extra nights lower down and an easy first day up high. For the detail on symptoms and what to do, read [dealing with altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/).

## The route at a glance

Sleeping altitudes are approximate. Towns spread over a range of heights, and sources differ by tens of metres.

- **Nights 1–2:** Santa Cruz, about 400 m
- **Nights 3–5:** Sucre, about 2,790 m
- **Nights 6–7:** Potosí, roughly 4,000 m or a little over
- **Night 8:** Uyuni, about 3,700 m
- **Nights 9–10:** on the 3-day salar and Lípez trip, the second night usually high in Sur Lípez
- **Night 11:** Uyuni, or straight on to La Paz
- **Nights 12–13:** La Paz, about 3,640 m in the centre
- **Night 14:** Isla del Sol, close to 4,000 m, or Copacabana, about 3,840 m

## Days 1–2: Santa Cruz

Santa Cruz de la Sierra is Bolivia's largest city and sits at about 400 m. Arriving here instead of La Paz means you land, sleep off the flight and adjust to the time zone without altitude on top. It is warm, green and a very different Bolivia from the one on postcards.

Use the second day to rest and sort practicalities: local cash, a SIM card, and confirming your onward flight.

## Days 3–5: Sucre

Fly up to Sucre, at about 2,790 m. That is just above the 2,750 m line the WMS mentions, so take the first afternoon slowly: walk, drink water, skip the alcohol. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html) advises avoiding alcohol for the first 48 hours at high altitude and keeping exercise mild.

Sucre is Bolivia's constitutional capital, with whitewashed colonial streets that are easy to wander at a gentle pace. Three nights here is not padding. It is the most important acclimatisation stop on the whole trip. See [Sucre and Potosí: Bolivia's colonial heart](/blog/sucre-and-potosi/) for what to do while you adjust.

## Days 6–7: Potosí

The road from Sucre climbs to Potosí, one of the highest cities in the world. Wikipedia gives its nominal elevation as about 4,070 m, and other sources put it nearer 4,090 m. Either way, this is a jump of well over 1,000 m in sleeping height, which is more than the guidelines suggest. This is where the CDC's advice to add extra acclimatisation days applies, which is why the plan gives you two nights and an easy arrival day.

Potosí grew rich on silver from the Cerro Rico, the mountain above the city, and its colonial centre and the mountain are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. On the second day, if you feel well, walk the colonial centre at an easy pace. If you have a headache with nausea, poor sleep or dizziness, do not go higher. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) advises going 300 to 1,000 m lower if symptoms don't improve after a day, and descending straight away with any confusion, poor balance or breathlessness at rest.

## Day 8: Potosí to Uyuni

The road drops slightly to Uyuni, at about 3,700 m. Uyuni is a rail junction and the main gateway to the salt flat, and it is not a place most people linger. Arrive, check your tour details, buy water and snacks, and get an early night.

## Days 9–11: the salar and Lípez

The classic 3-day trip runs from the Salar de Uyuni south through the Eduardo Avaroa reserve to the coloured lagoons and geysers near the Chilean border. Day one crosses the salt, often with a stop at the cactus island that gives this post its photo. The first night is usually in a basic lodge near the salar, at roughly salt-flat height (around 3,650–3,700 m).

The second night is the hard one. Lodges in Sur Lípez sit high and cold. Some trips stop near Laguna Colorada, which is at about 4,278 m. The exact stop varies by operator, so ask yours where you will sleep. Day three usually starts before dawn at the Sol de Mañana geyser field, at about 4,900 m and often the highest point of the trip, before turning back towards Uyuni or dropping you at the Chilean border.

There are [no official minimum standards for tour operators](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security), according to the UK government, so choose a reputable one and check your insurance covers the trip. Our [3-day tour explainer](/blog/uyuni-salt-flats-3-day-tour/) covers vehicles, drivers and what to pack, and [wet season or dry season](/blog/uyuni-wet-or-dry-season/) covers when to go.

## Day 12: to La Paz

From Uyuni, fly or travel overland to La Paz. Flights between the two exist but routes and timetables change, so check with the airlines close to your dates. The overland alternative is a long road journey, or the train north from Uyuni to Oruro and then the road on to La Paz. Passenger services are limited and timetables change, so confirm with the rail operator close to your date.

The centre of La Paz sits at about 3,640 m, slightly below where you have been sleeping. After a week and a half at altitude, the city should feel far easier than it would on day one.

## Day 13: La Paz

Ride the [cable cars](/blog/la-paz-cable-cars/), which give you the views across the city without the climbing. If you prefer, swap this for a day trip to the ruins of Tiwanaku, a UNESCO World Heritage Site about 70 km from the city.

## Day 14: Copacabana and Isla del Sol

Head to Lake Titicaca, whose surface sits at about 3,812 m. Copacabana, the lakeshore town, is at about 3,841 m, and boats leave from there for Isla del Sol. Yumani, the main village at the island's southern end, sits close to 4,000 m. Spend the night on the island or back in Copacabana, then return to La Paz for your flight home, or cross into Peru. Our [Lake Titicaca guide](/blog/lake-titicaca-bolivian-side/) has the detail.

## Alternatives and swaps

**Add the Amazon.** Add three or four days at the end: fly from La Paz to Rurrenabaque for the rainforest or the pampas. Finishing low is a pleasant way to recover before the long flight home. The [CDC](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/bolivia) recommends yellow fever vaccination for travellers aged 9 months and over going to areas below 2,300 m east of the Andes, and notes malaria transmission below 2,500 m. Rules on yellow fever certificates differ between sources, so check the CDC page and the [UK entry requirements](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/entry-requirements) before you travel. See [into the Bolivian Amazon](/blog/bolivian-amazon-rurrenabaque/).

**Swap the order.** You can run the route backwards: La Paz first, then the lake, then Uyuni, Potosí and Sucre. It works, but you sleep high from the first night. The CDC notes that in La Paz some travellers can descend below the airport's height to sleep for a night or two before going up again. If you go this way, plan two or three easy days on arrival, and talk to a doctor or travel clinic about acetazolamide, a prescription medicine the CDC suggests considering when abrupt ascent can't be avoided.

**Fly more, ride less.** Domestic flights save whole days, for example Uyuni to La Paz. Overland travel is cheaper and gives you the landscape, but the [UK government warns](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) that road travel can be dangerous, that many buses fall below UK standards, and that protest blockades can appear without warning. Travel by day where you can and choose better-known companies.

**Add Peru or Chile.** The Lípez trip can end at the Chilean border for San Pedro de Atacama, and Lake Titicaca is the usual overland link to Puno and Cusco. See [combining Bolivia and Peru in one trip](/blog/bolivia-and-peru-in-one-trip/).

## Before you book

- **Build in a spare day.** Blockades, weather and a bad night at altitude all happen. One unallocated day protects the rest of the plan.
- **Ask for sleeping heights in writing.** A good local operator should be able to tell you where you sleep each night and roughly how high it is.
- **Check the official advice close to departure.** Read the [FCDO travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia) and your own government's equivalent shortly before you fly, and again before long road legs.
