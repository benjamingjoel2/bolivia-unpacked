The cabin swings gently out of the station and the city falls away beneath your feet. Within a minute you are gliding over flat rooftops, washing lines and football pitches painted on concrete, with the whole of La Paz spread across the canyon walls around you. Ahead, the rim of the Altiplano draws closer. Behind you, on a clear day, the snowfields of Illimani fill the far side of the sky.

For the people who live here, this is the commute. For a visitor, it is one of the best views in South America, and one of the easiest ways to understand how this extraordinary city is put together.

## A city that needed a different kind of transport

La Paz fills a steep canyon, with its centre at roughly 3,600 m. Above it, on the flat plateau of the Altiplano, sits El Alto, a separate city at about 4,000 m, as the entries on [La Paz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Paz) and the [geography of Bolivia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Bolivia) explain. For decades, getting between the two meant crawling up switchback roads in minibuses and traffic.

The answer was to go over the top. Mi Teleférico, the cable car network, opened its first line, the Red Line, in May 2014. The manufacturer, Doppelmayr, completed the network with the Silver Line in March 2019, when it described the system as the world's biggest urban ropeway network, with ten lines covering roughly 33 km, according to a [2019 industry report](https://www.snowsportsnews.com/articles/doppelmayr-completes-the-world-s-biggest-urban-ropeway-network).

Today the [official Mi Teleférico site](https://www.miteleferico.bo/) lists ten lines, each named after a colour: Roja (red), Amarilla (yellow), Verde (green), Azul (blue), Naranja (orange), Blanca (white), Celeste (sky blue), Morada (purple), Café (brown) and Plateada (silver). Several of them climb between the canyon and El Alto. Others run across the city or entirely within El Alto, according to the [Mi Teleférico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mi_Telef%C3%A9rico) entry.

## What you see from the cabins

The lines are designed for getting around, not for sightseeing, which is exactly why riding them is so interesting. You see the city as it actually lives:

- **The canyon itself.** From the air you can see how La Paz pours down the slopes, and how sharply the canyon edge drops away beneath El Alto.
- **Rooftop life.** Courtyards, rooftop terraces, half-built upper storeys, market stalls and school playgrounds pass beneath you.
- **The mountains.** On clear days, the snow peaks of the Cordillera Real, including Illimani, line the horizon.
- **El Alto.** At the top, the city changes character: flatter, busier and bigger than many visitors expect.

Early morning and late afternoon light tends to be the most dramatic, and the views from the upper stations can be spectacular after dark, too. Weather on the Altiplano changes quickly, so take a warm layer even on a sunny day.

## How to use it as a visitor

The system is simple to use, but the details change, so check before you ride.

- **Fares and payment.** We do not give prices. Fares and payment options are set by the operator and change over time. Check the [official site](https://www.miteleferico.bo/) or ask at a station.
- **Opening hours.** The official site lists opening hours for each line, with shorter hours on Sundays. Check them before you plan an evening ride.
- **Changing lines.** Many stations are shared between two lines, so you can link several lines into one journey. Check how fares work when you change lines.
- **Station services.** The official site lists facilities at individual stations, which vary. Some include toilets, cafés and taxi ranks.

Rush hours are busy, as you would expect on a network that so many people use to get to work. A mid-morning or mid-afternoon ride is calmer and gives you more chance of a cabin with a view on both sides.

## A suggested ride

This is an idea for a half-day loop, built from the line and station lists on the [official site](https://www.miteleferico.bo/). It links four lines and takes you from the centre up to El Alto, along the rim and back down through Sopocachi. Check that each line is running on the day.

1. **Red Line (Línea Roja): Central to 16 de Julio.** Start at Estación Central, in the former railway station on Avenida Manco Cápac. The line climbs past Estación Cementerio, beside the city's general cemetery, and up to Estación 16 de Julio in El Alto. This is the steepest and most dramatic view of the canyon.
2. **Silver Line (Línea Plateada): 16 de Julio to Mirador.** Ride along the rim through Faro Murillo to Estación Mirador, high on the edge of the plateau.
3. **Yellow Line (Línea Amarilla): Mirador to Libertador.** Drop back down into the canyon through Buenos Aires and Sopocachi to Estación Libertador.
4. **Then choose.** From Libertador, the Sky Blue Line (Línea Celeste) runs via Av. Poeta and Teatro al Aire Libre to Estación Prado, close to the city centre. Or the Green Line (Línea Verde) takes you down through Obrajes to Irpavi in the lower southern districts.

If you only have time for one ride, make it the Red Line up and back. It shows you the most of the city in the shortest time.

## The 16 de Julio market

Step out at the top of the Red Line on the right day and you are in the middle of one of the biggest street markets you are ever likely to see. The 16 de Julio fair in El Alto is held on Thursdays and Sundays, according to both [Global Voices](https://globalvoices.org/2013/07/26/finding-everything-and-anything-at-bolivias-16-de-julio-fair/) and a June 2026 report in [Los Tiempos](https://www.lostiempos.com/actualidad/economia/20260608/feria-mas-popular-alto-paso-dias-zozoba-bloqueos).

Global Voices describes stalls selling almost anything: furniture, books, vehicles and spare parts, electronics, farm and building tools, new and second-hand clothes, natural medicines and animals. The local saying is that you can find everything "from a pin to a tractor".

It is a working market rather than a tourist attraction, and that is the appeal. Wander, watch, and pick up a snack from a food stall if you feel like it. Do not expect a souvenir shop.

The market is also sensitive to wider events. Los Tiempos reported that road blockades in May 2026 near the toll road between La Paz and El Alto kept many traders away and cut sales. If there are protests or blockades in the area, give it a miss. A local operator can tell you how things look that week.

## Safety on and around the cable cars

The cabins themselves are a calm way to travel. Most of the risks are on the ground, around stations and in crowds.

The [FCDO's safety page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/safety-and-security) warns that petty crime is common in central La Paz, and that tourists, especially those travelling alone, are at risk of express kidnapping. It names the Cementerio General and Sopocachi areas as places where incidents have occurred. Both are on the suggested route above. That is not a reason to avoid them, but it is a reason to be alert.

A few sensible habits:

- **Keep valuables out of sight**, especially in the market and at busy stations. Carry only what you need.
- **Travel in daylight** if you are going to El Alto on your own, or go with a guide.
- **Use established or radio taxis** if you need a car between stations, as the FCDO advises, rather than hailing one on the street.
- **Know about fake police.** The FCDO warns that thieves impersonate police officers, and that you should never get into an unmarked police car.
- **Watch for protests.** The FCDO says protests can occur with little notice. Move away from large gatherings.

## Altitude on the way up

Riding the cable car takes no effort, which makes it easy to forget how high you are going. The stations in El Alto are several hundred metres higher than the city centre, and the [FCDO health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/bolivia/health) names La Paz as a place where altitude sickness is a risk.

If you have just arrived, the cable cars are a good way to see the city without climbing its steep streets. Just keep the trip short, walk slowly at the top, and do not plan a long market visit in El Alto on your first day. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/altitude-sickness/) advises not going higher if you have symptoms such as a headache, nausea or dizziness. Our guide to [dealing with altitude in La Paz](/blog/altitude-in-la-paz/) explains how to plan the first 48 hours.

## Before you ride

- Check fares, payment options and opening hours on the [official Mi Teleférico site](https://www.miteleferico.bo/).
- Save the cable cars for an easy acclimatisation day, and keep El Alto visits short at first.
- Go to the 16 de Julio market on a Thursday or Sunday, and check there are no blockades or protests nearby.
- Keep valuables hidden and stay alert around busy stations, especially in El Alto and Sopocachi.
- Take a warm layer, sunglasses and sun cream for the top.
