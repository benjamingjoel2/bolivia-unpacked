Much of what travellers want to see in Bolivia is above 3,500 m. La Paz is at about 3,640 m, its airport at about 4,061 m, Potosí at about 4,090 m and the high point of the Uyuni–Lípez trip at around 4,800–5,000 m. Planning for altitude is the single biggest difference between Bolivia and most other trips.

The [altitude ladder](/altitude/) shows every major stop at its height.

## What altitude sickness is

Acute mountain sickness (AMS) usually starts within a day or so of arriving high. The typical signs are headache with one or more of nausea, tiredness, dizziness and poor sleep. It normally improves over a few days if you stop going higher.

Two rarer conditions are emergencies:

- **High-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE)**: confusion, clumsiness, drowsiness.
- **High-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE)**: breathlessness at rest, a cough, sometimes with frothy or pink sputum.

With either, descend at once and get medical help.

## Guideline rate of ascent

The CDC Yellow Book and the Wilderness Medical Society guidelines give the same core rule. Above about 3,000 m:

- raise your **sleeping** altitude by no more than about 500 m a day;
- take a rest day, sleeping at the same height, every three to four days;
- never go higher with symptoms of altitude sickness.

Flying from sea level to La Paz cannot follow this rule. The practical answer is to rest for the first days and go no higher until you feel well.

## Ordering a route

- **Start low if you can.** Santa Cruz (about 400 m) and Sucre (about 2,790 m) are natural starting points. From Peru, a few days in Cusco or the Sacred Valley before Titicaca and La Paz serves a similar purpose.
- **Save the highest nights for later.** The Sur Lípez lodges and Cordillera Real high camps should come after time in La Paz or elsewhere above 3,500 m.
- **Use lower rest stops.** Coroico in the Yungas or Sucre make useful breaks between high places.

## Medicine

Acetazolamide is a prescription drug that can help prevent AMS when you cannot ascend slowly. Discuss it with a doctor or travel clinic before you travel. Coca tea is widely offered in Bolivia. The NHS and CDC do not list it as a proven prevention.
